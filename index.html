Why are wetlands important?

Biodiversity: Wetlands provide habitat for waterbirds, fish and other wildlife.

Water regulation: Wetlands can store water and help moderate flooding.

Ecosystem protection: Wetlands support important ecological processes and species.

Ramsar sites represent internationally important wetlands, not every wetland in Australia.

What does this map show? New South Wales has the highest number of Ramsar wetlands (13), followed by Victoria and Western Australia (12 each). The Northern Territory has fewer listed sites (2). Key finding: New South Wales ranks first for the number of Ramsar sites but sixth for total mapped area. In contrast, South Australia ranks fifth for site count but first for mapped area, while the Northern Territory rises from eighth to second. This reveals that having more internationally recognised wetland sites does not necessarily mean having greater mapped wetland coverage.

02. Why do wetlands matter?

Wetlands come in many forms, from freshwater swamps and lakes to coastal saltmarshes and mangroves. Each type contributes to Australia's diverse wetland ecosystems.

This visualisation compares the mapped area of different wetland types across Victoria. Larger rectangles represent greater wetland area. Hover over each rectangle for more information.

Key finding: Subtidal embayments occupy approximately 244,451 hectares in the Victorian Wetland Inventory, making them the largest mapped category by area. Temporary freshwater lakes also occupy a substantial area, at approximately 102,329 hectares. This highlights the diversity of Victoria's coastal and inland wetland environments.

Source: Victorian Government, Victorian Wetland Inventory (Current).

Key finding: Subtidal embayments cover approximately 244,451 hectares but consist of only 3 mapped polygons. In contrast, small dams and storages (under 8 ha) account for more than 363,000 mapped polygons, despite covering only about 56,766 hectares. This demonstrates that the number of mapped features does not necessarily reflect the total area they occupy.

Source: Victorian Government — Victorian Wetland Inventory (Current).

Key finding: Temporary freshwater lakes cover approximately 102,329 hectares in Victoria, compared with 30,332 hectares for permanent freshwater lakes. However, the opposite pattern appears for saline lakes, where permanent lakes cover approximately 79,181 hectares compared with 18,385 hectares for temporary lakes. This demonstrates how wetland permanence varies across ecosystems.

Source: Victorian Government — Victorian Wetland Inventory (Current).

03. Wetlands Under Pressure

Australia's inland water bodies experience significant fluctuations in water coverage. Changes in rainfall, drought and flooding can influence how much water is visible across the landscape.

Key finding: Australia's non-perennial inland water coverage fluctuated significantly between 1988 and 2024, reaching approximately 5.8 million hectares around 2011 before declining sharply in subsequent years. Perennial water coverage remained comparatively stable, although it also varied over time. These changes illustrate the variability of Australia's inland water environments.

Source: Australian Bureau of Statistics, National Land Cover Account, 2024. Note: Inland water coverage is not equivalent to total wetland extent.

Key finding: Between 1988 and 2024, Australia's mapped perennial inland water coverage increased from approximately 0.95 million to 1.95 million hectares, while non-perennial water coverage increased from approximately 3.07 million to 3.73 million hectares. However, these two observations do not indicate a continuous increase, as water coverage fluctuated substantially between these years.

Source: Australian Bureau of Statistics — National Land Cover Account, 2024. Note: Inland water coverage is not equivalent to total wetland extent.

04. Protecting Australia's Wetlands

Australia has designated wetlands of international importance under the Ramsar Convention. These sites vary considerably in their geographical size and location.

Key finding: Ramsar wetlands vary considerably in their mapped geographical area. Several large sites occur across northern and inland Australia, while many smaller sites are concentrated in the southeast. This demonstrates that a region with more Ramsar sites does not necessarily contain a greater total mapped wetland area.

Source: Australian Government — Ramsar Wetlands of Australia. Circle sizes represent summed mapped polygon areas, not independently verified designated-site areas.

Key finding: Ramsar site sizes vary considerably across Australia. The Northern Territory has relatively large mapped sites, while states such as New South Wales and Western Australia show a much wider range of site sizes, including unusually large outliers. This highlights why the number of Ramsar sites alone does not reflect their total mapped area. Note that the Northern Territory has only two sites, so its distribution should be interpreted cautiously.

Source: Australian Government — Ramsar Wetlands of Australia. Areas represent summed mapped polygon areas. The horizontal axis uses a logarithmic scale.

Key finding: South Australia has the greatest total mapped Ramsar area at approximately 2.35 million hectares, followed by the Northern Territory at around 2.13 million hectares. In contrast, New South Wales has the highest number of Ramsar sites but a much smaller total mapped area. This demonstrates that having more Ramsar sites does not necessarily mean having greater wetland coverage.

Source: Australian Government — Ramsar Wetlands of Australia. Areas represent summed mapped polygon areas, not independently verified designated-site areas.
